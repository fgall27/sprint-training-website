Sprint Interval Training: Benefits, Workouts, and How to Start Safely

You don't have to be a competitive athlete to explore faster running. Learn how intervals work, what the research says, and how to build speed progressively.

Sprint interval training is a demanding form of exercise that alternates very intense, short efforts with recovery periods. It can improve cardiovascular fitness, but full-speed sprinting isn't the best starting point for everyone.

If you're a recreational runner, gym-goer, or fitness beginner, the goal should be to build toward faster running gradually. You can benefit from interval workouts without immediately attempting all-out sprints.

Important distinction: The beginner workouts in this guide use controlled running intervals. They are preparation for sprint training, not laboratory-style all-out sprint interval training (SIT).

What Is Sprint Interval Training?

Sprint interval training, often abbreviated SIT, involves repeated short bouts of near-maximal or maximal effort separated by substantial recovery.

Research protocols commonly use very intense cycling efforts lasting around 20 to 30 seconds, although exact durations and methods vary. Running-based sprint training may use shorter distances and different rest periods.

Importantly, sprint interval training is not identical to every workout called HIIT. HIIT includes many forms of hard exercise that do not require all-out sprinting.

Training style Typical approach
Steady running Maintain a relatively consistent pace.
Running intervals Alternate faster running with slower running or walking.
HIIT Alternate demanding efforts with recovery; intensity and duration vary.
Sprint interval training Use short, extremely demanding efforts with substantial recovery.

For someone new to fast running, controlled intervals are generally a more sensible starting point than maximum-effort sprints.

What Are the Benefits of Sprint Interval Training?

1. Improved cardiovascular fitness

Research reviews have found that sprint interval training can improve aerobic capacity, often measured using VO2 max. This reflects the body's ability to use oxygen during demanding exercise.

However, sprint interval training isn't universally better than regular endurance training. Both approaches can improve cardiovascular fitness.

2. A different way to challenge your fitness

Running intervals introduce changes in pace that aren't present during steady jogging. This can offer variety and a different training challenge.

3. Potentially time-efficient conditioning

Some sprint interval protocols achieve fitness improvements with relatively little time spent exercising at high intensity. But warmups, rest periods, and cooldowns still count toward the total time required.

4. A path toward better running speed

Practicing faster running can help you become more comfortable with changes in pace. Developing maximum sprint speed also requires appropriate technique, sufficient rest, and progressive training.

Keep the bigger picture in mind: The CDC recommends adults accumulate at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity or 75 minutes of vigorous-intensity activity weekly, or an equivalent combination, plus muscle-strengthening activity on at least two days. Sprinting is not required to meet these recommendations.

Beginner Sprint Workouts: Where Should You Start?

If you're completely new to exercise, start by building a comfortable walking routine. Progress toward easy jogging before adding faster running.

If you can already jog comfortably and want to experiment with speed, try the following controlled interval workout.

Beginner Running Interval Session

Warmup: Walk briskly, then jog easily for 8-12 minutes. Add a few gentle, progressive pickups.

Main workout: Run faster than your usual easy pace for 10 seconds, then walk for 90-120 seconds. Repeat 4 times.

Effort: Comfortable-to-challenging, but controlled. Do not sprint all-out.

Cooldown: Walk easily for 5-10 minutes.

Frequency: Start with 1-2 nonconsecutive sessions per week, depending on your current fitness and recovery.

The purpose is to introduce faster movement without excessive fatigue. If the effort feels too demanding, reduce the pace or return to easier walk-jog intervals.

Choose a flat, unobstructed surface. Avoid attempting hard sprints on slippery ground, in heavy traffic, or when already fatigued.

A Four-Week Beginner Running Interval Plan

This example is for generally healthy adults who already tolerate easy jogging. It illustrates a gradual progression toward faster running, not a four-week all-out sprint program.

Week Faster running Walking recovery
1 4 x 10 seconds 90-120 seconds between efforts
2 5 x 10 seconds 90-120 seconds between efforts
3 5 x 12 seconds 90-120 seconds between efforts
4 5 x 15 seconds 90-120 seconds between efforts

Maintain a controlled running speed throughout the four weeks. Include a warmup and cooldown each session. Keep at least one recovery day between demanding running sessions.

This is an illustrative progression, not a clinically validated protocol. Repeat a week or reduce the workload whenever recovery, soreness, or running technique suggests you need more time.

Five Common Sprint Training Mistakes

  1. Starting at maximum speed. Build tolerance to faster running before attempting truly maximal efforts.
  2. Skipping your warmup. Increase your movement intensity gradually.
  3. Resting too little. Recover enough to maintain controlled, consistent movement.
  4. Progressing too quickly. Avoid increasing speed, repetitions, and training frequency simultaneously.
  5. Ignoring other exercise. Account for strength workouts, sports, and other demanding activities.
Safety: Stop exercising if you experience chest pain, faintness, or unusual severe shortness of breath, and seek appropriate medical care. Stop running if sharp pain occurs. If you have a cardiovascular condition, significant injury history, or concerns about vigorous exercise, consult a qualified healthcare professional before beginning.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sprint interval training good for beginners?

Maximum-effort sprint interval training can be unnecessarily demanding for beginners. Walking, easy jogging, and controlled running intervals provide more accessible starting points.

Can sprinting improve cardiovascular fitness?

Yes. Studies show that sprint interval training can improve aerobic capacity. But it is not the only effective form of cardiovascular exercise.

How many times per week should I do running intervals?

For the introductory plan above, start with one or two nonconsecutive sessions per week if your existing fitness allows. The appropriate frequency depends on your health, recovery, and other training.

Do I need a track?

No. A flat, safe, unobstructed path or suitable field can work for controlled running intervals. Use a track when consistent distances are important.

Is sprinting better than jogging?

Neither is universally better. Sprint intervals and steady jogging have different demands, benefits, and recovery requirements. The best choice depends on your goals and what you can perform consistently and safely.
