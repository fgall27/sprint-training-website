Proper Sprinting Form: 5 Drills to Run Faster

Learn how sprinting mechanics work, improve your acceleration, and practice five technique drills that athletes and everyday fitness enthusiasts can use.

Running faster isn't simply about moving your legs as quickly as possible. Efficient sprinting involves applying force to the ground, coordinating your arms and legs, and using appropriate body positions as you accelerate.

Whether you're a football player, recreational athlete, or someone who wants to become more athletic, understanding sprinting form gives you a useful foundation for speed training.

This guide breaks down the basics and introduces practical drills. No starting blocks or specialized sprinting equipment are required.

Important: There is no single perfect running style for every body. Effective sprinting form changes between acceleration and upright running, and individuals naturally move somewhat differently. The techniques below are general coaching principles, not rigid positions you must force yourself into.

Understanding Proper Sprinting Form

Sprinting has several phases, including the start, acceleration, upright running, and deceleration. Each involves slightly different movement demands.

Two especially important phases are acceleration and maximum-speed running.

Phase 1: Acceleration

Acceleration begins when you push away from a standing or moving start. Your first steps help overcome inertia and build forward speed.

During early acceleration, athletes typically use a forward-leaning body position and relatively short, powerful steps. The body gradually rises as speed increases.

Simple cue: Push the ground behind you and allow your body to rise naturally as you accelerate.

Phase 2: Upright Sprinting

As you approach higher speeds, your torso becomes more upright and ground contact times generally become shorter.

The goal is to maintain coordinated movement, active arm action, and effective foot contact without unnecessary tension.

Simple cue: Run tall, keep your shoulders relaxed, and avoid reaching far ahead with each foot.

These phase differences are discussed in sprint mechanics resources from the National Strength and Conditioning Association and World Athletics.

Five Key Techniques for Better Sprinting Form

1. Use the Right Body Position

During your first steps, a forward lean helps orient your push toward acceleration. This should come from your overall body position rather than excessively bending forward at the waist.

As you gain speed, let your posture become more upright. Trying to stay excessively low throughout a sprint can interfere with natural mechanics.

2. Coordinate Your Arm Movement

Your arms help balance and coordinate your running motion. Swing them mostly forward and backward, allowing your elbows to bend naturally.

Try to avoid excessive side-to-side movement or crossing your hands far across your torso.

3. Avoid Overstriding

Reaching your foot far ahead of your body can increase braking forces. During upright sprinting, aim for effective ground contact near your body's center of mass.

Don't deliberately shorten every step, either. Stride length and cadence should develop together as your mechanics and speed improve.

4. Keep Your Upper Body Relaxed

Sprinting requires substantial effort, but excessive tension in your neck, face, and shoulders can interfere with coordinated movement.

Think about using powerful leg and arm actions while keeping the upper body as relaxed as the movement allows.

5. Practice Gradual Acceleration

Speed builds progressively during a sprint. Your first steps should emphasize effective force application, not an immediate attempt to run with your longest possible strides.

Gradual acceleration also gives beginners an opportunity to practice technique below maximum intensity.

Five Sprinting Form Drills to Practice

These drills provide simple ways to practice individual parts of sprint technique. They are teaching exercises, not guaranteed shortcuts to greater speed. Actual sprint practice, recovery, and physical preparation also matter.

Drill 1

Standing Arm Drive

Purpose: Practice coordinated forward-and-backward arm movement.

How to do it: Stand tall with your elbows comfortably bent. Move your arms in opposite directions, keeping your shoulders relaxed and limiting excessive cross-body movement.

Starting amount: 2 sets of 15 seconds.

Common mistake: Tensing your shoulders or swinging both arms across the body.

Drill 2

Wall March

Purpose: Practice alignment and leg action associated with acceleration.

How to do it: Face a sturdy wall and place your hands against it. Lean forward with a relatively straight line through your torso and supporting leg. Slowly alternate lifting one knee while maintaining balance and posture.

Starting amount: 2 sets of 5 controlled movements per leg.

Common mistake: Bending excessively at the waist or arching your lower back.

Drill 3

A-March

Purpose: Practice posture, rhythm, and coordinated knee lift.

How to do it: Move forward with a deliberate marching pattern. Lift one knee, coordinate the opposite arm, and place your foot back underneath your body.

Starting amount: 2 x 10-15 yards at a controlled pace.

Common mistake: Leaning backward or rushing through the movement.

Drill 4

Falling Starts

Purpose: Practice initiating acceleration from a standing position.

How to do it: Stand tall with feet in a comfortable position. Allow a small forward lean from the ankles until you need to take a step. Accelerate smoothly for a short distance.

Starting amount: 2-3 controlled starts over 5-10 yards.

Common mistake: Throwing your upper body forward or losing balance before moving.

Drill 5

Progressive Sprint Build-Ups

Purpose: Practice transitioning from acceleration toward faster, more upright running.

How to do it: Begin at an easy running speed and gradually accelerate over approximately 20 yards. Finish at a controlled, comfortably fast pace rather than all-out speed.

Starting amount: 2-3 repetitions with generous walking recovery.

Common mistake: Accelerating too aggressively before your body is prepared.

Example Sprint Technique Workout

If you already tolerate regular running comfortably, you can combine a few drills into a short practice session. This example introduces sprint mechanics without requiring maximum-speed efforts.

Part Activity
Warmup 8-12 minutes of easy movement, dynamic mobility, and gradual running pickups
Arm action 2 x 15 seconds of standing arm drive
Posture 2 x 5 wall marches per leg
Coordination 2 x 10 yards of A-marching
Acceleration 2 x 10-yard controlled starts
Running form 2 x 20-yard progressive build-ups
Cooldown 5-10 minutes of easy walking

Take generous recovery between faster efforts, typically around 90-150 seconds or longer when needed. Stop the speed portion if your technique deteriorates.

New to sprinting? Start at controlled, submaximal speeds. High-speed sprinting places greater demands on muscles and tendons than ordinary walking or jogging. Build up over multiple sessions instead of immediately trying maximum-effort repetitions.

This is an illustrative practice session, not an individualized or clinically validated progression. If you're new to exercise generally, first establish a comfortable foundation of regular physical activity.

Common Sprinting Form Mistakes

  1. Trying to take the longest possible strides. This can encourage overstriding and unwanted braking.
  2. Forcing an exaggerated lean. Early acceleration involves a forward lean, but bending excessively at the waist is not the goal.
  3. Holding tension in the shoulders. Maintain active arm movement without unnecessary stiffness.
  4. Practicing maximum speed while fatigued. Recovery helps preserve movement quality during speed sessions.
  5. Changing too many things at once. Focus on one useful technique cue at a time and allow practice to become more natural.

It may help to record a short video of your running from the side so you can observe posture and movement without relying on how the sprint felt. Video alone does not provide a complete biomechanical assessment.

Safety: Choose a flat, unobstructed surface and warm up before faster running. Avoid hard sprinting while injured or unusually fatigued. Stop if sharp pain occurs. People with medical concerns or relevant injury histories should consult an appropriate healthcare professional before beginning demanding speed training.

Frequently Asked Questions

How can I improve my sprinting form?

Begin with consistent sprint practice at an appropriate intensity. Focus on posture, coordinated arms, gradual acceleration, and effective foot contact. Introduce drills gradually and prioritize quality over excessive repetitions.

Should I lean forward when sprinting?

A forward body position is characteristic of early acceleration. As you build speed, your torso naturally becomes more upright. You should not force the same lean throughout the entire sprint.

Should I run on my toes?

Sprinting generally involves relatively forefoot-oriented contact, but attempting to stay on your tiptoes is not a useful universal cue. Focus on controlled, coordinated running rather than forcing an unnatural foot position.

Can beginners learn proper sprinting form?

Yes. Beginners can practice posture, arm coordination, and controlled acceleration drills. Maximum-effort sprinting should be introduced only after building appropriate physical readiness and tolerance to faster running.

Will better form automatically make me faster?

Not necessarily. Sprint speed also depends on strength, force production, coordination, recovery, training history, and individual characteristics. Technique work is one part of a broader speed development program.
